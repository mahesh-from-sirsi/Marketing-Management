1. Meaning, nature, scope and importance of marketing

Marketing is the process through which organisations understand customer needs, create valuable offerings, communicate their benefits and make them accessible through exchanges. Marketing is not synonymous with advertising: advertising is one promotional activity within marketing.

Nature

Marketing is customer-oriented, exchange-based, dynamic, research-driven and coordinated across functions. Its scope includes consumer and industrial goods, services, ideas, experiences, people, places and non-profit causes.

Why it matters

Marketing helps identify demand, design useful offerings, set sustainable prices, build brands, select channels and retain customers. A company that makes an excellent product but cannot reach or satisfy customers may still fail.

Shimoga example: A neighbourhood millet-snack business interviews office workers before selecting pack sizes. The research is marketing; the Instagram promotion is only one part of the resulting plan.

These are conceptual orientations, not a strict historical ladder: firms may display several orientations simultaneously.

3. Core concepts of marketing

Needs are felt states of deprivation; wants are culturally and personally shaped ways of meeting needs; demands are wants backed by purchasing power and willingness. A commuter needs transport, wants a scooter and expresses demand only when able and willing to purchase one.

Market offerings include products, services and experiences. Value is the customer's assessment of benefits relative to costs and sacrifices. Exchange involves obtaining something of value by offering something in return. A market consists of actual and potential buyers of an offering.

Concept check: A student says, “Everyone needs an iPhone.” Explain why this is more accurately a want than a universal human need.

4. Types of needs

A useful classification distinguishes stated needs (what customers say), real needs (the underlying benefit), unstated needs (expected but not mentioned), delight needs (unexpected extras) and secret needs (personal motives not openly expressed).

For a budget hotel guest, “I want a cheap room” is stated. Safe, clean accommodation is a likely real need. Clean bedding is unstated; complimentary late checkout may delight; appearing like a savvy traveller could be a secret motive. Marketers should test assumptions rather than assume they can read customers' minds.

5. Functions of marketing

Major marketing functions include market research, demand forecasting, product and service planning, branding, pricing, promotion, channel selection, physical distribution, selling, customer support and relationship management.

Functions are interdependent: a discount campaign fails if inventory is unavailable; a good product may fail when distribution is weak; customer feedback may reveal the need to redesign packaging.

Practice: Map each function to a local bakery launching a ₹30 breakfast snack. Specify one observable decision and one metric for each function.

6. Marketing versus selling

DimensionMarketingSelling
Starting pointCustomer needs and market insightExisting product or offer
Primary focusCreating and delivering customer valueConverting prospects into buyers
Time horizonAcquisition, satisfaction and retentionOften immediate transaction
Typical measuresCustomer satisfaction, retention, market share, profitabilityConversions, sales volume, revenue

Selling is a legitimate and important component of marketing. The distinction is about scope and starting point, not “good marketing versus bad selling.”

7. Customer value, satisfaction and relationship marketing

Customer-perceived value reflects perceived benefits compared with monetary, time, effort and psychological costs. Satisfaction arises when experience meets or exceeds expectations. Relationship marketing seeks ongoing mutual value rather than treating every purchase as an isolated transaction.

Illustrative sequence: value proposition → purchase experience → satisfaction or dissatisfaction → trust and repeat choice → possible loyalty. Loyalty is not automatic; competitors, switching costs and circumstances matter.

Metric example: A retailer measures repeat purchase rate as customers who purchased again during a defined period divided by customers eligible to repeat. Define the period and customer cohort before interpreting the number.

The 4Ps are coordinated decisions, not four independent checkboxes. For services, an extended 7Ps framework may add people, process and physical evidence.

9. Micro and macro marketing environment

Micro environment: actors close to the organisation, such as the company, suppliers, intermediaries, customers, competitors and relevant publics. Macro environment: broader demographic, economic, natural, technological, political/legal and sociocultural forces.

Example: a café's supplier increasing coffee prices is a micro-level issue; inflation and shifts in disposable income are macro-level pressures. A competitor opening nearby is micro; changing food-labelling regulation is macro.

10. Techniques used in environment analysis

PESTLE reviews political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental forces. SWOT separates internal strengths and weaknesses from external opportunities and threats. Competitor analysis compares rivals' value propositions, prices, channels and positioning. Customer research uses interviews, observation, surveys and sales data.

Do not confuse SWOT's external opportunities and threats with internal strengths and weaknesses. State the evidence behind each finding and distinguish a fact from a prediction.

Application: A café plans online ordering. Technology adoption is a PESTLE factor; a trained delivery team is an internal strength; a rival's new delivery offer may be an external threat.

11. Marketing ethics and social responsibility

Ethical marketing requires truthful claims, transparent prices, fair treatment, respect for privacy, responsible targeting and avoidance of manipulative or discriminatory practices. Social responsibility considers the consequences of business decisions for customers, workers, communities and the environment.

Examples of risks include fabricated testimonials, undisclosed paid endorsements, misleading “limited stock” claims and collecting unnecessary personal data. A lawful practice is not automatically an ethical one.

12. Marketing to the 21st-century customer

Customers compare prices and reviews across channels, expect convenience, use digital platforms and may demand transparency about data and sustainability. Their journeys can move between discovery on social media, comparison on marketplaces, purchase in a shop and post-purchase service over messaging.

Modern marketing therefore combines customer insight, consistent omnichannel experience, responsible personalisation, responsive service and measurement. Avoid assuming all customers are digital-first: affordability, access, language and trust still shape choices.

Final integrative task: Design a launch plan for a local service using a customer need, segment, value proposition, 4Ps, PESTLE observation, ethical safeguard and measurable outcome.
